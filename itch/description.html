16 July 1942, Berlin. In a very restricted circle, the German Naval Staff hears that "at the Fuehrer's request, the Commander in Chief, Air Force is preparing a raid on New York by two BV 222's". The flying boats are to be refuelled in mid-Atlantic by U-boats. Twelve days later the war diary closes the matter in one sentence: the Luftwaffe's General Staff "declined to participate".

Two Flying Boats, 1942 is a hypothetical study built on those pages of the Naval Staff's war diary, read in the complete translation the US Navy made after the war. It is the third in a series with Iron Hammer, 1945 and Dragon's Lair, 1944–45. It does not ask whether a raid could have changed the war; it could not. It asks what the people who planned it had in front of them, what they counted, and what they did not.

Three layers of time, ten chapters.

  • The wish, 1938–41: Göring telling the aircraft industry in 1938 that he lacks "the bomber which flies with 5 tons of explosives as far as New York and back"; Hitler wanting the Azores in 1940 and 1941 as "the only facility for attacking America"; and the city as the Nazi leadership's own papers describe it: the seat of "the New York Jews".
  • The raid, July 1942: two BV 222 flying boats, two type IXc U-boats as tankers, a base at sea 1,000 km off the coast, hoses for one and a half to two hours, take-off at dusk, "not before 10 Sep." And the navy's price: two U-boat missions, "60,000 to 80,000 GRT of enemy shipping".
  • The city and the fear: New York in 1942, dimmed at night, with U-boats off its coast and refugees from Germany whose families were being deported; then the legend of a German aircraft off Long Island and the fear of rocket U-boats that ended, in April 1945, in Operation Teardrop and over 350 dead.

Two ways through. Read the study as a dossier, with the war diary and the Nuremberg documents in facsimile and transcription; the file's own plan is computed at the end. Or take the Naval Staff's seat: give the U-boats or refuse them, place Base 1, send one flying boat or two, choose the new moon in September or October, and decide whether the matter rests when the Luftwaffe declines. If it does not, a model flies your raid with the file's figures and named assumptions.

A careful line. The war diary names no purpose for the raid. The study shows, from the leadership's own documents, what "New York" meant to them in the same months, Rosenberg's note on "the New York Jews" of 14 December 1941 among them, but it does not put a motive into the file that the file does not contain.
